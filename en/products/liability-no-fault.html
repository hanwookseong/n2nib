Nullity of the contract

If the insured event has already occurred at the time the contract is made, the contract is void. However, where the contract is void due to the company's intent or negligence, or where the company knew or could have known of the nullity before acceptance yet did not refund the premium, the company refunds the premium with interest at the policy-loan rate published by the Korea Insurance Development Institute, compounded annually, for the period from the day after payment to the day of refund.

Losses not covered

The specific losses not covered (exclusions) are set out in each insurer's policy wording and product description; on this page, see the “Cover” tab(or the “Losses not covered” section) for the main exclusions. For other cover-specific grounds on which claims are not paid, please refer to the policy wording.

Cover start date

The company provides cover, in accordance with the policy, from the time it accepts the application and receives the first premium. Where the company accepts the application after receiving the first premium with it, cover also begins from the time the first premium was received.

Policyholder's handwritten signature

The application must be completed by the policyholder, and the policyholder and the insured must sign it by hand. Failure to sign by hand may result in disadvantages regarding the validity of the contract. On an internet cyber-mall, an electronic signature may be used instead.

Right to be informed and have the product explained

The policyholder has the right to be given and have explained the information needed about the product they wish to buy.

Duty to explain

The insurer and N2N Insurance Brokerage must explain the important matters of the product to ordinary financial consumers.

Duty of disclosure before the contract

When applying, the policyholder, the insured or their agent must disclose truthfully the facts they know regarding the questions in the application (including the questionnaire). Otherwise the claim may be declined or the contract cancelled. Where insurance is taken out by telephone or other means of communication, the duty is performed by answering the seller's questions, which are recorded, without a separate written questionnaire, so answers must be given with particular care.

Duty of disclosure after the contract

If, after the contract is made, any of the following arises in respect of the subject-matter insured, the policyholder or the insured must notify the company in writing without delay and obtain endorsement on the policy.

  • When intending to take out, or learning of, a contract with another insurer covering the same risk as this contract
  • When transferring the subject-matter insured
  • When altering, rebuilding or extending the subject-matter insured or the building housing it
  • When moving the subject-matter insured to another location
  • When the risk is, or is found to have been, materially changed

Withdrawal of the application

  • The policyholder may withdraw the application within 15 daysof receiving the policy, in which case the premium paid is refunded. However, a contract more than 30 days after application (45 days where a policyholder aged 65+ contracted by telephone) cannot be withdrawn.
  • In addition, a medical-examination contract, a contract with a cover period of 90 days or less, guarantee insurance, statutory compulsory insurance, liability insurance under the Automobile Accident Compensation Act, or a commercial-insurance contract concluded by a professional financial consumer cannot be withdrawn.

Quality-assurance scheme

  • If, after application, the policyholder did not receive the policy wording and their copy of the application, was not given an explanation of the important contents of the wording, or did not sign the application by hand, they may cancel the contract within 3 monthsof the contract being formed.
  • In that case the premium already paid is refunded to the policyholder, with interest at the policy-loan rate compounded annually for the period the premium was held.

Why the surrender value may be less than the premiums paid, or nil

The surrender value is the amount paid if the contract is cancelled early. Unlike bank savings, insurance combines risk protection and savings: part of the premium is paid out as claims to other policyholders who suffer accidents, and part covers the insurer's operating expenses, so the surrender value on early cancellation may be less than the premiums paid, or nil.

Depositor protection

  • This policy is protected under the Depositor Protection Act, such that the surrender value (or the maturity benefit) plus other payments is protected up to “KRW 100 million per person” (aggregated with the insurer's other protected products).
  • Separately, the aggregate accident-claim amount of that insurer's protected products is “KRW 100 million per person” protected.
  • (However, a policy whose policyholder and premium payer is a corporation is not protected.)

Tax benefit (protection-type insurance)

Under Article 59-4(1) of the Income Tax Act (special tax credit), for protection-type insurance taken out by an employee only, a tax credit of 12% of the premium paid (capped at KRW 1 million per year) is available. Tax matters may change with amendment or repeal of the relevant tax law.

Sectors that need no-fault compensation

Five sectors where statutory strict liability applies

💊

Pharma / bio (post-market)

Drug adverse-reaction relief and similar pharma/bio areas. Strict liability applies without proof of fault. (The pre-market clinical-trial stage is a separate "clinical-trial liability" wording area.)

🥫

Food manufacturing / distribution

Strict liability for harmful food under the Food Sanitation Act. An area of strict liability for food poisoning, foreign objects and missing allergy labels.

🚗

Vehicle manufacturing / transport

Operator strict liability under the Automobile Accident Compensation Act. A compulsory-insurance area with statutory limits.

🏭

Chemicals / environment

Strict liability under the Environmental Damage Relief Act, the Chemical Substances Control Act and the like. The site-environmental-pollution accident area.

⚡

New technology / new products

Industrial-convergence new-product certified operators. A new-product category combining compulsory cover and the no-fault area.

A dispute pattern seen in the field

At a food maker, a consumer suffered an allergic reaction because the allergy label was missing on some products. The maker held an ordinary PL policy, but with strict liability for harmful food under the Food Sanitation Act operating at the same time, liability could well be recognised without going through the defect/fault-proof process. Had a no-fault compensation wording been in place separately, cover could have been reviewed in the strict-liability area — a case that shows the need to combine ordinary PL with a no-fault wording in sectors where strict liability applies, such as food and pharma.

Three things easily missed when buying no-fault compensation insurance

The wording and structure points decision-makers most often overlook

  • 1

    Check the scope of strict liability

    The law and scope of strict liability differ by sector. Confirm in advance which law applies to your site — drug adverse-reaction relief, the Food Sanitation Act, the Automobile Accident Compensation Act, the Environmental Damage Relief Act — and combine the wording accordingly.

  • 2

    The gap between the statutory limit and actual loss

    In areas with a statutory limit, such as the Automobile Accident Compensation Act and industrial-convergence new products, the compulsory limit alone often cannot cover the actual single-accident loss. Supplementing with an excess-limit endorsement or ordinary PL is common.

  • 3

    The narrow definition of exclusions

    Because liability is recognised without proof of the operator's fault, the strict-liability area has narrowly defined exclusions. War/terror/political risk, the user's clear misuse and certification/legal breaches are the usual exclusions; understand clearly at the application stage that the exclusion scope is more limited than in ordinary PL.

Frequently asked questions

The questions decision-makers ask most when considering no-fault compensation insurance

What is no-fault compensation insurance?

It is a wording covering the "strict liability" area, where liability is recognised on the mere fact of loss without proof of the operator's fault. It is close to essential in sectors where strict liability applies by law — pharmaceutical adverse-reaction relief, strict liability for harmful food under the Food Sanitation Act, the Guarantee of Automobile Accident Compensation Act, and compulsory cover for industrial-convergence new products.

Which sectors does it apply to?

The main sectors are pharmaceutical makers/importers (drug adverse-reaction relief), food manufacturing/distribution (strict liability for harmful food under the Food Sanitation Act), vehicle manufacturing/transport (Automobile Accident Compensation Act), industrial-convergence new-product certified operators, and some chemicals/environment-related sectors. The core targets are areas where statutory strict liability is a mandatory rule, all in the post-market (post-commercialisation) area. Subject harm during a pre-market clinical trial is a separate "clinical-trial liability" wording area.

How does it differ from ordinary PL?

Ordinary PL recognises liability after proof of a defect and causation under the Product Liability Act. No-fault compensation insurance covers the strict-liability area where liability is recognised on the mere occurrence of loss without proof of fault; it is usually designed in combination with ordinary PL so both cover areas are addressed.

What exclusions are there?

Typical exclusions include political risks such as war, civil commotion and terrorism, natural disasters (other than as separately provided by law), the operator's intent, the user's clear misuse or self-harm, and certification/legal breaches (operating without certification). Because strict liability has narrowly defined exclusions, the exclusion clauses must be checked closely by insurer.

Is it compulsory?

Whether it is compulsory differs by legal area. Drug adverse-reaction relief, the Automobile Accident Compensation Act and industrial-convergence new-product certification are statutorily compulsory (or effectively so), while strict liability for harmful food under the Food Sanitation Act is voluntary but high-risk. In compulsory areas, non-compliance can affect the business licence or the validity of certification.

How is the limit designed?

Where a statutory compulsory limit applies, a compulsory limit + excess-limit endorsement is common. In voluntary areas the design considers turnover, the maximum single-accident loss (e.g. a mass food incident or a vehicle accident) and cumulative risk, and the limit options differ with the insurer's underwriting result.

How is the premium assessed?

The insurer assesses it on the sector (pharma, food, vehicles, new products, etc.), turnover, export share, past no-fault claim history and the QC/safety-management system. The exact premium and whether cover can be accepted are confirmed after underwriting by insurers such as AIG, Chubb, DB, KB, Meritz and Hyundai.
